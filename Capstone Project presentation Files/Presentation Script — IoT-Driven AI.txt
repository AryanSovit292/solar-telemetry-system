(~20 sec)

Slide 2 — The Real-World Problem: Grid Instability

Slow down here — this is the "why should anyone care" slide.

"Let's start with the problem. India is investing heavily in solar power, but solar generation is inherently unpredictable. A cloud passing overhead, a sudden monsoon shift, or just normal weather variation can cause generation to swing wildly within minutes.

The tools grid operators rely on today — standard weather forecasts — are macro-level. They tell you the weather for an entire city or region, but they completely miss the micro-climate right at a specific solar plant.

This mismatch between what's predicted and what's actually happening causes real problems: frequency drops, blackouts, and — because operators can't trust the forecast — they're forced to keep expensive, polluting fossil-fuel backup plants running 'just in case.' That's the problem we set out to fix."

(~45 sec)

Slide 3 — The Solution: Real-Time IoT Telemetry + AI

This is your thesis statement slide — say it with confidence.

"Our solution has two halves working together. First, hyper-local edge sensing — we deploy physical IoT devices directly at the solar site, so we're capturing the actual conditions on the ground, not a regional guess.

Second, we feed that continuous stream of real sensor data — irradiance, temperature, and electrical output — into a deep learning model, specifically an LSTM, or Long Short-Term Memory network, which is very good at learning patterns in sequences over time. That lets us forecast generation several hours ahead, using real ground-truth data instead of generic weather APIs."

(~40 sec)

Slide 4 — Integrated IoT Hardware Architecture

This is the technical core — speak like you built it, not like you're reading a spec sheet.

"Let's get into the hardware. At the center is an ESP32 microcontroller. We chose it because it has built-in Wi-Fi, enough pins to talk to all our sensors at once, and it's cheap and low-power enough to leave running at a plant site continuously. It acts as our edge device — reading every sensor and pushing the data out wirelessly.

Then we have three sensors doing three different jobs:

An irradiance sensor measures exactly how much sunlight is hitting the panel — that's the 'fuel' for generation, and our strongest predictive signal.
A DS18B20 temperature sensor tracks the panel's surface temperature — because a hot panel is actually a less efficient panel, so this corrects for that.
And an INA219 sensor measures the panel's actual current and voltage output — this is our ground truth, the real number we're trying to forecast.

Together, these three signals give our AI model everything it needs to understand why generation is behaving the way it is — not just that it changed."

(~60–70 sec)

Slide 5 — End-to-End IoT-to-AI Data Pipeline

Walk through this like a flow — use your hand to gesture left-to-right if presenting in person.

"Here's how data actually flows through our system, in three stages.

Step one, edge collection — the ESP32 reads all three sensors every minute and pushes that data into a local MySQL database.

Step two, data processing — a Python script pulls those records, filters out night-time hours where there's no generation to predict, and scales the data into windows the model can learn from.

Step three, AI prediction — our trained LSTM network takes those recent sequences and produces a rolling forecast of expected solar output, which gets logged for visualization."

(~45 sec)

Slide 6 — Model Evaluation Metrics

This is where you prove rigor — don't rush the comparison point at the end, it's your strongest selling line.

"To know if our model is actually good, we evaluate it with two standard metrics. RMSE, Root Mean Squared Error, which heavily penalizes big mistakes — important because a large forecasting error is exactly what causes grid imbalance. And MAE, Mean Absolute Error, which tells us the average size of our errors across all daylight hours.

But the number that matters most to us is the comparison: when we tested our IoT-based approach against relying on a public weather API alone, our local sensor telemetry produced significantly lower error rates. That's the core proof that hyper-local hardware actually makes a measurable difference."

(~40 sec)

Slide 7 — Software Output: Power BI Control Dashboard

This is the "show, don't tell" slide — if you have a screenshot or live dashboard, gesture to it here.

"All of this feeds into a live Power BI dashboard that acts as an operational control room. Operators can see a side-by-side chart of actual generation from our IoT sensors versus what our AI predicted — so they can immediately see how well the model is tracking reality.

We also built in proactive alerting — if there's a sudden weather shift or a drop in panel efficiency, the dashboard flags it visually, so operators can react before it becomes a grid problem."

(~35 sec)

Slide 8 — Delivered Business & Engineering Value

Land the "so what" here — connect back to Slide 2's problem statement explicitly.

"So what does this actually deliver? Three things. Grid resilience — operators get an early warning window to spin up storage or backup reserves before a drop happens, not after. Cost reduction — because forecasts are more trustworthy, plants can rely less on expensive fossil-fuel backup generation. And carbon efficiency — more clean solar energy gets reliably integrated into the grid, which directly reduces emissions.

In short, this turns solar from an unpredictable variable into a resource the grid can actually plan around."

(~40 sec)

Slide 9 — Project Implementation Roadmap

Close strong and confident — this is your last impression.

"Finally, here's our implementation roadmap. Phase one is hardware and data: programming the ESP32, wiring up the pyranometer and temperature sensors, setting up our local MySQL database, and collecting baseline training logs.

Phase two is AI and dashboard: training and tuning the LSTM network, validating it with RMSE and MAE, automating the full pipeline, and deploying the live Power BI control interface.

That's our project — a complete pipeline from a $10 sensor on a solar panel, all the way to an AI-powered forecast that could help stabilize India's grid. Thank you — we're happy to take any questions."

(~35 sec)

Working of sensors;

1. ESP32 Microcontroller

Why: It's the only piece that needs to talk to all three other sensors at once — it has built-in Wi-Fi (so no separate networking module), enough GPIO pins to support analog, I2C, and OneWire protocols simultaneously, and it's cheap and low-power enough to leave running at the plant site continuously.
How it works: It runs a firmware loop that reads all sensors every minute, packages the readings into a timestamped JSON payload, and pushes it out over Wi-Fi (via MQTT) into your MySQL database.

2. Irradiance Sensor

Why: Solar output is almost directly proportional to sunlight intensity — it's the single strongest predictive signal for the AI model. Without it, the model has no way to "see" the sunlight driving generation, only the result.
How it works: A photodiode/pyranometer-style sensor outputs a voltage proportional to incoming solar radiation. The ESP32's ADC reads this as a raw value (0–4095), which gets converted to W/m² using a calibration curve built against a reference pyranometer.

3. DS18B20 (Temperature Sensor)

Why: Panels lose efficiency as they heat up (~0.4–0.5% per °C above 25°C). Two panels under identical sunlight can produce different power if one is hotter — without temperature data, the model can't explain that variance.
How it works: It's a digital sensor communicating over the OneWire protocol — it has its own internal ADC, so it returns temperature directly in °C with no external calibration needed, unlike the irradiance sensor.

4. INA219 (Current/Power Sensor)

Why: This is your ground truth — the actual value the LSTM is trying to forecast. Everything else (irradiance, temperature) are predictors; this measures what's really being generated right now.
How it works: It sits in series with the panel's output and measures the tiny voltage drop across a built-in shunt resistor to calculate current, plus separately measures bus voltage — both read over I2C. Power is then just V × I.

The logic tying them together: irradiance = the cause, temperature = the correction factor, electrical output = the effect being measured and forecasted, and the ESP32 = the glue that captures all three in sync.
